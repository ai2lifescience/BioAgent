Explain what an open reading frame is and when an ORF is biologically useful. Do not search the web.

An open reading frame (ORF) is a contiguous stretch of nucleotide sequence that begins with a start codon (usually ATG, coding for methionine) and ends with one of three stop codons (TAA, TAG, or TGA), with no intervening stop codons in the same reading frame.

Key characteristics
Since DNA is translated in triplets (codons), there are three possible reading frames per strand — six total when considering both strands.
An ORF must be a multiple of 3 nucleotides between start and stop.
In a random sequence, a stop codon occurs roughly every 21 codons, so any ORF longer than ~100 codons (~300 nt) is unlikely to occur by chance.
When ORFs are biologically useful

1. Gene discovery in newly sequenced genomes Long ORFs are the primary signal for identifying protein-coding genes. A stretch with a strong Kozak context, no internal stops, and length >100 codons strongly suggests a real gene.

2. Finding alternative translation products uORFs (upstream ORFs) in the 5′ UTR of eukaryotic mRNAs can regulate translation of the main gene. Some encode short regulatory peptides.

3. Identifying small proteins or micropeptides Many functional small proteins (<100 aa) were historically overlooked because they fall below the length cutoffs used in genome annotation. ORF detection with ribosome profiling data is now a standard way to find them.

4. Detecting frameshifts or sequencing errors If an annotated coding sequence unexpectedly harbors a stop, it can signal a frameshift mutation or a base-calling error in the sequence.

5. Comparative genomics (phylogenetic footprinting) ORFs conserved in syntenic regions across species are strong candidates for functional coding elements, even if they encode hypothetical proteins.

6. Designing expression constructs Knowing the ORF boundaries lets you clone the full CDS into an expression vector without truncation or inclusion of 5′/3′ UTRs.

In short: ORF analysis is the first pass at finding what a genome encodes — it's cheap, fast, and surprisingly powerful — but needs supporting evidence (homology, expression data, conservation) to confirm biological function.
