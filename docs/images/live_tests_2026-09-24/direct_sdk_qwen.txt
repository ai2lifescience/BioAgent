Explain what an open reading frame is and when an ORF is biologically useful. Do not search the web.

What an ORF is An open reading frame is a stretch of DNA or RNA with no in-frame stop codons, beginning at a start codon (usually ATG in DNA, AUG in mRNA) and ending at the first in-frame stop codon (TAA, TAG, or TGA). Because codons are read triplets, every nucleotide sequence actually has six possible reading frames — three on each strand — and any frame that yields a continuous run of codons without a stop is a candidate ORF. In practice, "ORF" usually implies the longest such stretch, often required to exceed a minimum length (e.g., 90+ bp) to be worth considering.

When an ORF is biologically useful A long ORF is meaningful evidence of a protein-coding region when several signals align:

Length: It encodes a plausible protein; most real genes produce proteins of roughly 100+ amino acids, and very long ORFs in otherwise coding context strongly suggest genuine genes.
Start/stop context: The start codon is followed by an in-frame run without premature stops, and often sits in a Kozak-like consensus (eukaryotes) or a Shine-Dalgarno ribosomal binding site (prokaryotes).
Expression evidence: Corresponding RNA-seq coverage, conserved sequence across related species, or proteomic peptide matches confirm it's actually translated.
Domain structure: The predicted peptide contains recognizable functional domains or motifs (e.g., transmembrane segments, signal peptides, catalytic residues).

When it isn't Not every ORF is a gene. Random DNA produces short open frames by chance (expected maximum ORF length grows only slowly with sequence length), pseudogenes and frameshifted regions can leave long ORFs that are never expressed, and regulatory or structural RNAs encode no protein at all. So an ORF is a candidate — a hypothesis of a protein-coding sequence — and becomes biologically useful when corroborated by expression, conservation, and functional motif evidence. That's why ORF-finding is a standard first step in genome annotation, especially for novel or unsequenced organisms where annotation can't be borrowed from known genes.
