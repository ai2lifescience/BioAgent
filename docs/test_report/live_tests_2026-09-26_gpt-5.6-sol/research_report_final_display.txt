# Phage therapy for multidrug-resistant bacterial infections

**Evidence currentness date: 26 September 2026**

## Executive summary

Phage therapy is biologically plausible and often effective in laboratory and animal models, including against multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms and biofilms. However, the reviewed clinical evidence does **not establish efficacy**. Uncontrolled compassionate-use cases cannot separate phage effects from surgery, antibiotics, natural recovery, or selection bias; preclinical findings likewise cannot establish benefit in patients.

The strongest direct evidence is PhagoBurn, a small randomized phase 1/2 trial in *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* burn wounds. Phage treatment reduced bacterial burden more slowly than standard care, and the trial stopped for insufficient efficacy. However, phage titre declined after manufacture: patients received approximately **10² PFU/mL instead of the intended 10⁶ PFU/mL**. Thus, PhagoBurn neither supports efficacy nor cleanly tests an adequately potent formulation.

Safety findings are generally reassuring but too sparse to exclude uncommon harms. Key barriers include strain-specific matching, titre stability, dosing, resistance, biofilm delivery, manufacturing standards, and regulation of personalized or changing cocktails.

## Methods

PubMed searches covered randomized trials, systematic reviews, clinical research, biofilm infections, and MDR organisms. Searches targeting WHO, FDA, NIH, and ClinicalTrials.gov returned no usable sources during this run.

The synthesis therefore uses eight PubMed-indexed sources:

- One randomized trial
- Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

- Clinical-case evidence
- Preclinical evidence

Study type, pathogen or infection, outcomes, and limitations were compared. Full-text appraisal and formal risk-of-bias assessment were not performed.

## Findings by evidence tier

### Randomized clinical evidence

PhagoBurn provides the strongest causal evidence but did not demonstrate benefit over standard care. Its negative result is clinically important, although the product was substantially under-strength because the titre declined from the intended 10⁶ PFU/mL to approximately 10² PFU/mL.

This four-log discrepancy and low-dose resistance make formulation stability and potency central limitations. Future trials need:

- Release and bedside potency testing
- Stable formulations
- Susceptibility testing or “phagograms”
- Justified dosing
- Clinically meaningful endpoints

The historical reassessment also cautions against assuming efficacy. Although older studies appeared favourable, four analyzable modern trials collectively indicated non-effectiveness relative to controls. Cross-era heterogeneity and the small modern evidence base prevent firm conclusions.

### Observational clinical evidence

Reviews describe apparently successful treatment of refractory Gram-positive, wound, implant, and fracture-related infections. These cases support feasibility and hypothesis generation.

However, patients commonly also received debridement and systemic antibiotics. Without controls, the contribution of phages cannot be isolated. Selective publication of dramatic successes is also plausible.

### Preclinical evidence

Animal and laboratory evidence is consistently encouraging:

- The *S. maltophilia* meta-analysis reported a large survival association and apparent dose dependence.
- Hydrogel studies suggest that local matrices can protect phages and extend tissue residence.
- Staphylococcal and oral-biofilm studies support antibacterial activity.
- Phage–antibiotic synergy may occur.

These results cannot establish human efficacy. Animal inocula, dosing, treatment timing, immune context, and delivery may not reproduce established MDR infections in patients.

## Safety

PhagoBurn reported adverse events in 3 of 13 phage recipients versus 7 of 13 controls, with no treatment-related deaths. However, it was too small and underdosed to establish safety at therapeutic titres.

Other reviews reported no major adverse effects, but safety reporting was sparse. Current evidence is reassuring yet inadequate to exclude:

- Rare toxicity
- Inflammatory reactions
- Immune neutralization
- Manufacturing contaminants
- Ecological effects
- Adverse effects from bacterial lysis

## Practical and regulatory challenges

- **Matching and resistance:** Narrow host ranges require rapid isolate testing; resistance may emerge, especially with inadequate exposure.
- **Dose and formulation:** Stability, storage, delivery vehicles, pharmacokinetics, and tissue penetration require standardization.
- **Biofilms and anatomy:** Delivery differs across wounds, implants, lungs, bloodstream, and bone.
- **Combination therapy:** Possible antibiotic synergy complicates causal evaluation.
- **Manufacturing:** Cocktails require standardized identity, purity, potency, and screening for undesirable genes or contaminants.
- **Trial design:** Personalized susceptibility complicates conventional randomization, although adaptive or platform trials remain possible.
- **Regulation:** Compassionate-use successes do not replace reproducible products and comparative trials.

## Evidence gaps

Research priorities include:

- Adequately powered randomized trials in defined MDR infections
- Verified potency at administration
- Dose-ranging and pharmacokinetic studies
- Standardized phagograms
- Clinical cure, recurrence, and mortality endpoints
- Resistance and immune-response monitoring
- Systematic adverse-event reporting
- Trials separating phage effects from surgery and antibiotics
- Comparisons among monophages, cocktails, engineered phages, enzymes, and delivery matrices

## Conclusion

Phage therapy remains a promising **experimental strategy**, not a proven treatment for MDR bacterial infection.

Preclinical activity is substantial, and compassionate-use experience supports feasibility. However, neither uncontrolled cases nor preclinical studies establish efficacy. Controlled evidence is sparse and not convincingly positive.

PhagoBurn was inferior on its primary endpoint, although severe underdosing from titre decay also made it an inadequate test of a potent, stable formulation. Clinical adoption requires standardized manufacturing and susceptibility testing, reliable dose and formulation control, and well-powered comparative trials.

## Limitations of this report

- The synthesis is restricted to eight PubMed abstracts; full texts, protocols, and supplementary data were unavailable.
- Reviews may contain overlapping primary studies, so the eight sources are not eight independent clinical datasets.
- Most evidence is preclinical or uncontrolled.
- MDR status was not consistently reported.
- Trusted web searches yielded no usable records; no claims from WHO, FDA, NIH, or ClinicalTrials.gov were included.
